# General Investigation Framework

## 1. Project Overview

The General Investigation Framework is an evolving, reusable method for investigating information, extracting structured knowledge, evaluating evidence, analysing relationships, identifying meaningful changes, and producing useful, evidence-based conclusions.

The project aims to establish a systematic way of moving from an investigation question to a well-supported understanding of a subject.

It is not tied to one industry, research topic, document type, or reporting format. The same principles should be applicable to different domains, including business, public policy, regulation, markets, organisations, and other complex systems.

The framework is being developed through practical experience. Existing methods and templates will be documented first, then reviewed and improved where there is a clear reason to do so.

## 2. Why This Project Exists

Information is often scattered across documents, websites, reports, datasets, announcements, regulations, and other sources.

Finding information, however, does not automatically produce understanding.

A useful investigation must establish:

- What question are we trying to answer?
- What information is needed to answer it?
- Where can relevant information be found?
- What does each source actually establish?
- Which claims are supported, uncertain, disputed, or unverified?
- How does the information connect to other entities, events, conditions, and dependencies?
- What has changed compared with what was previously known?
- What consequences might follow, and what evidence supports those possibilities?
- What remains unknown, and what should be investigated next?

The framework exists to make this process systematic, traceable, repeatable, and understandable to someone other than the original researcher.

## 3. Main Objective

Develop a practical investigation method that transforms scattered information into structured, verified, connected, and decision-useful knowledge.

The framework should help a researcher move beyond collecting facts to understanding how those facts relate, what has changed, why a change might matter, and which conclusions the evidence can reasonably support.

## 4. Scope of the Framework

The project initially focuses on four connected areas.

### 4.1 Investigation method

How to define an investigation question, identify useful sources, examine source material, extract evidence, verify claims, assess limitations, and identify unanswered questions.

### 4.2 Information structure

How to organise findings so that information remains traceable and can be connected across sources, entities, relationships, events, and time periods.

### 4.3 Analysis and interpretation

How to identify changes, trace dependencies, examine possible transmission mechanisms, distinguish evidence from inference, assess implications, and determine what further investigation is required.

### 4.4 Reporting and communication

How to turn the investigation into a clear output, such as a weekly review, a focused deep dive, or a longer analytical report, without losing the evidence and reasoning behind the conclusions.

These areas are related but should not be treated as interchangeable. A reporting template is not the entire investigation method, and the investigation method should not depend on a single reporting format.

## 5. General Investigation Model

The initial working model is:

**Investigation question → Source discovery → Source examination → Evidence extraction → Verification → Structured information → Relationship analysis → Change analysis → Implication assessment → Reporting**

The process begins by defining what we need to understand. We then locate and examine relevant sources, extract and verify what they establish, organise the findings, examine how the information connects, assess changes and possible consequences, and communicate the results with appropriate evidence and uncertainty.

This is a working model, not yet a final or fully validated procedure. The detailed steps, feedback loops, and decision points will be refined as the project develops.

Investigations may need to return to earlier stages. For example, analysing a relationship may reveal that a source is missing, a definition is unclear, or a claim needs further verification.

## 6. Core Principles

All parts of the framework should follow these principles.

1. **Start with a clear question.** Research should have a defined purpose rather than becoming an unstructured collection of information.

2. **Examine sources systematically.** Understand what a source is, why it exists, what it covers, and what limitations affect its use.

3. **Separate sources from claims.** The presence of a statement in a document does not automatically establish that statement as true.

4. **Prioritise evidence.** Conclusions should be traceable to identifiable supporting material.

5. **Distinguish facts from interpretation.** Direct evidence, verified findings, inferences, hypotheses, and unknowns must not be silently blended together.

6. **Preserve context and time.** Record relevant publication dates, event dates, effective dates, discovery dates, definitions, scope, and source status.

7. **Organise information for reuse.** Findings should be recorded consistently so that they can be compared, connected, and revisited.

8. **Analyse relationships and dependencies.** Information should not be treated as isolated facts when its significance depends on connections to other entities or conditions.

9. **Test possible consequences.** A plausible mechanism is not proof that an outcome has occurred. Identify supporting evidence, assumptions, and missing links.

10. **Track changes carefully.** Distinguish a new event from newly discovered information, a changed source, a changed interpretation, or the absence of a material change.

11. **Record uncertainty and gaps.** An unanswered question is a legitimate research result when it is clearly documented.

12. **Improve the method deliberately.** Changes to the framework should solve an identified problem or improve its usefulness, not simply increase the number of files or procedures.

## 7. Existing Work

The project builds on work already developed through practical investigations.

### 7.1 Source investigation guide

A practical guide has been drafted to explain how to investigate different information sources, including PDFs, spreadsheets, word-processing documents, presentations, databases, websites, and scanned images.

It includes a detailed twelve-step investigation checklist, source classifications, common mistakes, verification considerations, a standard findings record, and quality checks.

This material should be preserved and organised in the project rather than recreated from scratch.

### 7.2 Existing investigation question and information structure

A recurring investigation approach has been used to identify what information is needed, organise extracted findings, examine entities and relationships, trace dependencies, assess changes, and determine possible implications.

The original question and the precise structure used in earlier work must be documented before a replacement is proposed. Improvements should be explained in terms of the problems they solve.

### 7.3 Existing reporting structure

A recurring weekly research and reporting structure has also been used. Additional analysis, including deeper document investigations, has developed alongside that work.

The earlier structure and its evolution need to be documented accurately. Where the historical details are uncertain or unavailable, they should be marked for confirmation rather than reconstructed as if they were verified.

## 8. Planned Project Organisation

The project will initially use four substantive Markdown files.

- `README.md` — project purpose, context, principles, current status, and navigation.
- `01-investigation-method.md` — the investigation process, detailed source checklist, source-type guidance, evidence verification, and quality controls.
- `02-investigation-question-and-analysis-structure.md` — the original investigation question, information requirements, structured analysis, relationship analysis, change analysis, and proposed improvements.
- `03-reporting-structure-and-evolution.md` — the existing reporting structure, how it developed, and recommended improvements.

Each file should contain enough context and explanation for a new reader to understand the subject without needing to consult the conversation in which it was developed.

Additional files should only be introduced when the material becomes too large, has a distinct purpose, or benefits from being maintained separately.

## 9. Development Approach

The framework will be developed incrementally.

1. Document the project purpose and general principles.
2. Preserve and organise the existing source investigation guide.
3. Document the original investigation question and information structure.
4. Document the earlier reporting method and its evolution.
5. Identify gaps, overlaps, ambiguities, and weaknesses.
6. Propose improvements and explain their rationale.
7. Test the method against a practical worked example.
8. Revise the documentation based on what the test reveals.

The existing material is the starting point. The objective is to improve it carefully, not discard it or redesign it without justification.

## 10. Current Status and Open Questions

### Established direction

- The project is a general investigation framework, not a domain-specific system.
- The existing source investigation checklist should be preserved.
- The investigation question, analysis structure, and reporting structure should be documented separately enough to avoid confusion.
- The project should begin with a small number of substantive files.
- The method should distinguish evidence, inference, uncertainty, and unknowns.
- No automation or software implementation is required at this stage.

### Still to be established

- The exact original wording and scope of the recurring investigation question.
- The precise details of the earlier weekly structure.
- Which elements of the later, more detailed analysis are essential to the general method.
- Which proposed improvements solve genuine problems and which are unnecessary.
- How best to test whether another person can follow the framework independently.

## 11. Definition of Progress

Progress is not measured by the number of files created.

The project is progressing when:

- The existing work is documented accurately.
- A new reader can understand the method without relying on undocumented context.
- Each major step has a clear purpose and explanation.
- Findings can be traced to evidence.
- Uncertainty and assumptions are visible.
- Relationships and possible implications are analysed systematically.
- The reporting format communicates conclusions without obscuring how they were reached.
- Practical testing identifies where the method works and where it needs improvement.

The long-term objective is a clear, reusable, evidence-based investigation method that can be applied across different subjects and adapted to different research tasks.
