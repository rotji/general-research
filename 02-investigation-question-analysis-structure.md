# Investigation Question and Analysis Structure

## 1. Purpose of This Document

This document explains how to turn an investigation question into a structured process for collecting, organising, and analysing information.

It covers:

- How to define the investigation question.
- How to determine what information is needed.
- How to identify the entities, conditions, events, and relationships relevant to the question.
- How to organise extracted information into a usable structure.
- How to identify changes and investigate their possible implications.
- How to distinguish established findings from interpretations and unanswered questions.
- How to improve the existing approach without losing useful work.

This document complements `01-investigation-method.md`.

The first document explains how to investigate sources and assess their evidence. This document explains what we are investigating, what information we need, and how the information should be organised and analysed.

## 2. The Central Idea

An investigation should not be organised around documents alone. It should be organised around the question we are trying to answer.

Documents, reports, datasets, announcements, and other sources provide information. They are inputs to the investigation, not necessarily its final structure.

The investigation question determines what information matters, what relationships need examination, which changes are relevant, and what conclusions the evidence needs to support.

For example, collecting ten documents about a subject does not automatically explain how that subject works, what has changed, or why a change might matter.

A structured investigation connects information from different sources to a common question and preserves the evidence behind the conclusions.

## 3. Defining the Investigation Question

### 3.1 What is an investigation question?

An investigation question defines what we want to understand, establish, explain, compare, or evaluate.

A good question gives the research a clear direction while leaving room to discover information that was not known at the beginning.

It should be specific enough to guide research but broad enough to capture relevant relationships and consequences.

### 3.2 Characteristics of a useful investigation question

A useful question should establish:

1. **Subject:** What system, issue, event, organisation, market, process, or situation are we investigating?
2. **Objective:** What do we want to understand or establish?
3. **Scope:** Which boundaries matter, such as geography, time period, population, process, or activity?
4. **Information needs:** What kinds of evidence would help answer the question?
5. **Analytical purpose:** Are we describing a situation, explaining how it works, identifying changes, evaluating consequences, or supporting a decision?
6. **Uncertainty:** What is not yet known and therefore requires investigation?

Not every question needs all six elements explicitly written into a single sentence. They should, however, be considered when defining the investigation.

### 3.3 Types of investigation questions

Questions can serve different purposes.

- **Descriptive:** What is happening, and what is the current situation?
- **Structural:** What are the main components, participants, resources, and processes?
- **Relational:** How are the components connected?
- **Explanatory:** Why did something happen, and through what mechanism?
- **Change-oriented:** What has changed compared with an earlier state?
- **Impact-oriented:** What consequences might follow, for whom, and under what conditions?
- **Evaluative:** How well is a process, policy, system, or intervention working?
- **Decision-oriented:** What does the evidence suggest should be considered or done next?

One investigation may include several of these question types. The primary question should still be clear. These categories help describe the task; they do not, by themselves, determine a complete method.

### 3.4 Selecting analytical methods

After clarifying the question, decide what reasoning or procedure would actually produce a satisfactory answer. Start with the required answer, not with a favourite method or a fixed checklist.

Ask:

1. What would a complete and useful answer need to establish?
2. What observations, records, measurements, definitions, or comparisons would support that answer?
3. What kind of reasoning is needed to move from the evidence to the answer?
4. Which methods are necessary, which might help, and which would add no meaningful value?
5. What could make the answer wrong or incomplete, and how could that be tested?

Possible methods include, but are not limited to:

- **Definition and conceptual analysis:** clarify meanings, categories, assumptions, and distinctions.
- **Descriptive analysis:** establish what exists or what is happening.
- **Comparison and evaluation:** compare options, cases, outcomes, or performance against explicit criteria.
- **Causal analysis:** investigate why an outcome occurred and test alternative explanations.
- **Quantitative analysis:** calculate, estimate, measure, or test patterns using appropriate data and methods.
- **Structural and relationship analysis:** identify components, roles, connections, and dependencies.
- **Process analysis:** establish how an activity or system operates, including steps, inputs, outputs, and failure points.
- **Historical and change analysis:** compare states across suitable time periods or reference points.
- **Spatial analysis:** investigate location, distance, distribution, or geographic relationships when relevant.
- **Scenario and forecasting analysis:** examine possible future outcomes, assumptions, and conditions.
- **Procedural or design analysis:** develop a workable process or solution against stated requirements and constraints.

This is a menu of methods, not a mandatory list. A question may need one method or several. Some methods may be combined, and the appropriate method may change when the investigation reveals new evidence. Record why a method is being used when the choice materially affects the conclusions. Do not force a question into an unsuitable method merely because that method appears in this document.

## 4. From the Question to Information Requirements

Once the question is defined, the next task is to determine what must be known to answer it.

This step prevents research from becoming an unstructured search for anything related to the subject.

### 4.1 Identify the information categories

Ask what information would be necessary to understand the subject and answer the question.

Depending on the investigation, the categories may include:

- Current state and background.
- Relevant entities and their roles.
- Processes, activities, and operating conditions.
- Relationships and dependencies.
- Rules, policies, constraints, and requirements.
- Resources, costs, incentives, and risks.
- Events, decisions, and changes over time.
- Measured outcomes and performance indicators.
- Effects on different participants or parts of the system.
- Exceptions, limitations, uncertainties, and missing evidence.

These are candidate categories, not a mandatory checklist for every project. Include only what is relevant to the question.

### 4.2 Turn categories into answerable subquestions

Each information category should generate one or more subquestions.

For example:

| Information category | Example subquestion |
|---|---|
| Structure | What are the main components of the system? |
| Roles | Who performs each relevant function? |
| Relationships | How do the components depend on one another? |
| Rules | Which requirements or constraints govern the process? |
| Change | What is different from the previous state? |
| Mechanism | How could this change affect another component? |
| Evidence | What sources establish the relevant facts? |
| Uncertainty | What information is still missing? |

These subquestions help determine what to search for, what to extract, and how to assess whether the investigation is complete enough for its purpose.

### 4.3 Define what would count as an answer

For each important subquestion, identify the evidence needed to answer it.

An answer might require an official record, a clearly defined dataset, several independent sources, a comparison over time, or evidence that a proposed mechanism actually operates.

If the necessary evidence cannot be found, the correct result may be an unresolved question rather than an unsupported conclusion.

## 5. Establishing the Baseline

For investigations that examine changes, a baseline is the best-supported representation of the relevant state of the subject against which later information can be compared. It is not everything known about the subject; it includes the information relevant to the question and comparison.

The baseline may describe:

- What was previously known.
- Which entities and relationships were established.
- What rules or conditions applied.
- What values or outcomes were measured.
- Which uncertainties already existed.
- Which claims had not yet been verified.

A baseline provides a reference point for later comparison.

Without one, it is easy to mistake old information for new developments, repeat previously established findings, or overstate the significance of a newly discovered source.

The baseline should be dated and supported by evidence. It should not be treated as complete merely because it is the first version.

## 6. Structuring the Information

Information should be organised so that each finding can be understood independently while remaining connected to the wider investigation.

### 6.1 Sources

Sources are the documents, datasets, websites, records, statements, or other materials from which information is obtained.

Record enough detail to identify the source and locate the relevant evidence.

A source may contain multiple claims, and different sources may support, contradict, or qualify the same claim.

### 6.2 Findings

A finding is a recorded statement about what the available evidence establishes or supports.

A useful finding should identify:

- What is being claimed.
- Which evidence supports it.
- Where the evidence can be found.
- When the claim or evidence applies.
- What scope or limitations affect it.
- Whether it is established, qualified, disputed, inferred, or unresolved.

A finding is not automatically a fact simply because it has been entered into a register.

### 6.3 Entities

An entity is a relevant person, organisation, institution, product, asset, location, process, system component, or other identifiable participant or object.

Record entities when their identity, role, activity, or connection matters to the investigation.

Not every named item in a source needs its own record. The purpose is to preserve useful structure, not to create unnecessary administration.

### 6.4 Relationships

A relationship describes a relevant connection between entities or between an entity and a process, event, condition, or resource.

Examples include:

- Supplies.
- Regulates.
- Depends on.
- Funds.
- Implements.
- Competes with.
- Is subject to.
- Is affected by.
- Reports to.
- Enables or constrains.

The existence of a relationship must be supported by evidence or explicitly labelled as a hypothesis.

### 6.5 Events and changes

An event is something that occurred or was reported to have occurred. A change describes a difference between states, conditions, rules, relationships, or measured outcomes.

A reported event may be verified, partially verified, or unverified. Its consequences must be assessed separately.

### 6.6 Unresolved questions

An unresolved question records something important that cannot yet be answered adequately.

It should state what is missing, why it matters, what evidence could resolve it, and what should be investigated next.

Keeping uncertainty visible prevents gaps from being silently filled with assumptions.

### 6.7 Keep analytical objects distinct

The following concepts are related but should not be treated as interchangeable:

- **State:** what is true or known about a subject at a particular time.
- **Event:** something that happened or is reported to have happened.
- **Change:** a difference between two states or conditions.
- **Relationship:** a connection between entities, conditions, processes, or resources.
- **Finding:** what the available evidence establishes or supports within a defined scope.
- **Interpretation:** what one or more findings mean when considered together.
- **Implication:** what may follow if a proposed mechanism and its necessary conditions hold.

For example, an acquisition is an event. The ownership before and after the acquisition describes a change in state. The buyer and acquired company are entities, and the acquisition connects them through a relationship. Evidence establishing the acquisition supports a finding. A conclusion that the buyer may gain greater market influence is an interpretation; a claim that competitors will lose access to distribution is a possible implication that requires additional evidence. The implication should not be reported as an observed outcome unless it has been established.

## 7. Analysing Relationships and Dependencies

Once relevant information has been structured, examine how its components connect.

The purpose is to understand how the system operates, which components depend on others, where constraints exist, and through what mechanisms a change could affect something else.

Questions to ask include:

- Which entities interact directly?
- What does each entity depend on?
- What resources, permissions, rules, or conditions enable an activity?
- Which components could constrain or interrupt another component?
- Which relationships are directly supported by evidence?
- Which relationships are inferred or still hypothetical?
- What other conditions would need to hold for an expected effect to occur?

A chain of plausible connections is not sufficient proof of a real-world outcome. Each important link should be examined, and missing links should be made explicit.

## 8. Analysing Changes Over Time

Change analysis compares the current evidence with a documented baseline or another appropriate reference point.

For each potential change, establish:

1. What was the earlier state?
2. What is the newer state?
3. What evidence supports the difference?
4. When did the change occur or take effect?
5. When was it announced or published?
6. When did the investigation discover it?
7. Is it a new event, newly discovered information, a changed source, or a changed interpretation?
8. What is the scope of the change?
9. Which entities, relationships, or conditions could be affected?
10. What evidence would establish whether the possible consequences occurred?

Useful working classifications include:

- `NEW_WORLD_EVENT` — a newly occurring real-world event supported by evidence.
- `NEWLY_DISCOVERED` — information about an event or condition that already existed but was not previously captured in the investigation.
- `SOURCE_CHANGED` — a source, record, or published position has changed.
- `INTERPRETATION_CHANGED` — the understanding of existing evidence has changed.
- `NO_MATERIAL_CHANGE` — the review found no material change within its stated scope.
- `UNVERIFIED` — a reported change has not been adequately established.

These classifications should be used carefully. A new article about an old event is not necessarily a new real-world event. Likewise, failure to find a change during a limited search does not prove that no change occurred.

## 9. From Change to Possible Implications

Identifying a change is only the beginning of impact analysis.

The investigation must explain how the change could affect another entity, activity, condition, or outcome.

For each possible implication, record:

- **Change:** What has been established?
- **Affected component:** What could be affected?
- **Mechanism:** How could the effect be transmitted?
- **Conditions:** What else must be true for the effect to occur?
- **Evidence:** Which parts of the mechanism are supported?
- **Assumptions:** Which parts remain inferred or hypothetical?
- **Expected outcome:** What could happen if the mechanism operates?
- **Observed outcome:** Is there evidence that it has already happened?
- **Uncertainty:** What remains unknown?
- **Next test:** What information would strengthen or weaken the explanation?

For example, a change in a formal requirement may create a new obligation for an organisation. Whether that obligation changes its costs or operations depends on its actual applicability, implementation, existing practices, and other conditions. Those effects should not be asserted without evidence.

## 10. Distinguishing Evidence, Inference, and Hypothesis

The framework should make the status of important statements visible.

### Evidence-supported finding

A claim supported by identifiable evidence within a defined scope.

### Inference

A conclusion derived from available evidence through reasoning. The reasoning and assumptions should be stated.

### Hypothesis

A plausible explanation or possibility that still requires testing.

### Unresolved question

A question for which the available evidence is insufficient to reach a responsible conclusion.

### Contradicted or disputed claim

A claim challenged by relevant evidence or by credible sources that cannot yet be reconciled.

These categories are not merely labels. They determine how strongly a conclusion can be presented and what additional research is needed.

## 11. Prioritising What to Investigate Next

Not every gap deserves equal attention.

Prioritise unresolved questions by considering:

- How important the answer is to the main investigation question.
- How much the answer could change the current interpretation.
- How uncertain the existing evidence is.
- Whether the question affects multiple entities or relationships.
- Whether reliable evidence is reasonably obtainable.
- Whether the result could alter a practical decision or recommendation.

The next research step should target an important uncertainty or test a consequential link, rather than simply collect more information.

## 12. Assessing Whether the Investigation Is Sufficient

An investigation does not need to eliminate every uncertainty to be useful.

It should, however, make clear what it has established and what remains unresolved.

Before reporting, ask:

- Have the main question and its scope been clearly defined?
- Have the important information categories been examined?
- Are the main findings traceable to evidence?
- Have relevant definitions, dates, exceptions, and limitations been considered?
- Are important relationships supported or appropriately qualified?
- Have changes been compared with a suitable baseline?
- Are proposed consequences supported by a plausible, examined mechanism?
- Are facts separated from inferences and hypotheses?
- Are the most important unanswered questions recorded?
- Is the conclusion proportionate to the strength and coverage of the evidence?

If important evidence is missing, the report should state that limitation rather than imply completeness.

## 13. Proposed General Analysis Sequence

The following is an adaptive sequence for organising an investigation. It is a guide to decisions, not a requirement to perform every analytical operation in every case.

1. **Clarify the task:** define the question, intended use, scope, constraints, and what would count as a satisfactory answer.
2. **Determine answer requirements:** break the question into subquestions and identify what must be established for each.
3. **Select methods:** choose the analytical methods needed to produce and test the answer. Record why specialist methods are necessary when that choice matters.
4. **Plan the evidence:** identify the required source types, data, observations, comparisons, or tests.
5. **Gather and examine evidence:** use `01-investigation-method.md` to examine relevant sources and preserve traceability.
6. **Structure the findings:** record the relevant findings and, where useful, entities, relationships, events, states, measurements, and uncertainties.
7. **Analyse and test:** apply the selected methods; examine alternatives, contradictions, assumptions, and limitations where relevant.
8. **Assess answer sufficiency:** determine whether the evidence answers the question to the required standard. If not, identify the specific gap and return to the relevant earlier step.
9. **Prepare the appropriate output:** present the answer, evidence, reasoning, uncertainty, and any requested implications or actions in a form suitable for the task.
10. **Review quality:** check that conclusions are proportionate to evidence, the scope is clear, and important limitations are visible.

Not every investigation needs a baseline, change classification, relationship map, calculation, forecast, or recommendation. Use these only when the question calls for them. Simple questions may need a short path through the sequence; complex questions may involve multiple loops between evidence gathering, analysis, and testing.

### 13.1 Answer sufficiency and stopping

An investigation is sufficiently complete when it has answered the question to the level justified by the available evidence and the task's needs, and when material limitations are made explicit. This does not require eliminating every uncertainty.

Continue investigating when an unresolved issue could materially change the answer, when a central claim lacks adequate support, or when a necessary comparison or test has not been performed. Stop or report a qualified answer when additional evidence is unlikely to change the conclusion, cannot reasonably be obtained, or falls outside the agreed scope. State which situation applies rather than implying certainty or completeness without justification.

## 15. Testing and Improving the Framework

The framework should be tested against questions that differ in purpose, complexity, domain, and required output. Useful test types include a conceptual question, a causal question, a comparison, a process investigation, a structural or relationship question, and a scenario-based question.

For each test, assess:

- Did the framework clarify what the question requires?
- Did it select appropriate methods without forcing unnecessary steps?
- Did it identify the evidence and reasoning needed?
- Did it expose important assumptions, uncertainty, or missing information?
- Did it produce an answer or output suitable for the task?
- Could another researcher follow the reasoning and reproduce the important checks?
- What specific failure or friction should be corrected?

Record the problem observed, the proposed change, and the expected benefit. Do not add methods, categories, or documentation merely because they seem potentially useful. Add or revise them when testing reveals a meaningful gap or when a clearly justified need arises.

The framework is intended to evolve through practical testing, while preserving a clear distinction between established principles, working proposals, and methods that have not yet been validated.

## 16. Intended Outcome

This document should help a researcher move from a question to a structured body of evidence and analysis.

It should explain not only what information to collect, but why that information matters, how its relationships should be examined, how changes should be assessed, and how conclusions should remain connected to the evidence.

The framework will be considered successful when it helps produce investigations that are systematic, transparent, repeatable, appropriately cautious, and useful beyond the original research task.
