# Reporting Structure and Evolution

## 1. Purpose of This Document

This document explains how investigation findings are organised and communicated through reports.

It addresses:

- The purpose of a recurring research or intelligence report.
- The distinction between the investigation process and its final report.
- The weekly reporting approach used in our previous work.
- The relationship between recurring reviews and deeper investigations.
- How findings, changes, evidence, relationships, implications, and unresolved questions should be presented.
- Potential improvements to the reporting structure.
- How to evaluate whether a report is useful, accurate, and sufficiently supported.

The goal is to develop a reporting approach that can be applied across different subjects without making the framework dependent on one industry or research topic.

**Important:** This document records the general reporting principles and a proposed structure. The exact historical weekly template must be confirmed against the earlier work before the proposed structure can be treated as a faithful record of the original.

## 2. Why Reporting Structure Matters

An investigation may contain many sources, findings, relationships, changes, and uncertainties. A report must organise this information so that a reader can understand the important results without having to reconstruct the entire research process.

A useful report should answer five broad questions:

1. What is the current understanding of the subject?
2. What has changed since the previous review or baseline?
3. What evidence establishes the important findings?
4. Why might the changes matter, and through what mechanisms?
5. What remains uncertain, and what should be investigated next?

The report should make the important conclusions visible while preserving enough supporting information to allow the reader to assess their reliability.

A report is not simply a summary of everything found during research. It is a structured presentation of the findings most relevant to the investigation question.

## 3. The Investigation and the Report Are Different

The investigation is the process used to discover, examine, verify, connect, and interpret information.

The report is one output of that process.

The investigation may involve numerous sources, detailed evidence checks, comparisons, calculations, alternative explanations, and unresolved questions. The report selects and organises the results according to the reader's needs and the purpose of the investigation.

A report should not replace the underlying evidence records. Instead, it should make important conclusions traceable to those records and their supporting sources.

The same investigation may produce different outputs, such as:

- A recurring weekly review.
- A focused investigation of one event or change.
- A detailed document or policy analysis.
- A comparative analysis.
- A decision briefing.
- A final research report.

These outputs may differ in length and organisation while relying on the same core principles of evidence, traceability, and transparent reasoning.

## 4. The Role of a Recurring Review

A recurring review examines the available information at a defined interval and compares it with what was previously known.

Its purpose is not to repeat the entire background investigation every time. It is to identify meaningful developments, revise previous interpretations when necessary, and determine whether additional investigation is justified.

A recurring review should distinguish:

- New events.
- Newly discovered information about existing events or conditions.
- Changes to existing sources or records.
- Changes in the interpretation of existing evidence.
- Developments that have been reported but remain unverified.
- Reviews in which no material change was established within the scope examined.

A recurring review should retain relevant context, but it should not present old information as new simply because it appears in a newly published source.

## 5. Proposed General Weekly Reporting Structure

The following structure is a working proposal informed by our previous investigation work. It should be compared with the original weekly template before being treated as the definitive historical version.

### Section 1. Executive Intelligence Brief

**Purpose:** Present the most important conclusions and changes at the beginning of the report.

This section should explain:

- The current overall assessment.
- The most important verified developments.
- Why those developments matter.
- The most consequential uncertainty.
- Whether the overall assessment has changed since the previous review.

The section should prioritise significance rather than the number of sources found.

A strong executive brief allows the reader to understand the main result without reading every supporting section. Its statements must still be supported by the detailed evidence and analysis that follow.

### Section 2. Change Log

**Purpose:** Record what has changed since the previous review or established baseline.

For each material change, record:

- A short description of the change.
- Its classification.
- The event date, publication date, effective date, and discovery date where relevant.
- The supporting source or evidence record.
- The previous understanding.
- The current understanding.
- The significance of the change.
- Any verification still required.

The change log should distinguish new events from newly discovered information and changed interpretations.

Repeated coverage of the same event should not automatically generate multiple event records.

### Section 3. Priority-Event Investigations

**Purpose:** Examine important developments in greater depth.

For each selected development, explain:

1. What is reported or known to have happened?
2. What evidence establishes the event?
3. Who or what is directly involved?
4. What is the status of the event or decision?
5. What remains unverified?
6. Why does the event merit further investigation?
7. What additional evidence would clarify its significance?

Not every reported event needs a detailed investigation. Priority should depend on its relevance, potential significance, uncertainty, and ability to change the current assessment.

### Section 4. Relationship and Implication Analysis

**Purpose:** Explain how a verified change could affect other parts of the subject being investigated.

This section should identify:

- The component directly affected.
- The relevant relationships and dependencies.
- The possible transmission mechanism.
- Conditions that could strengthen, weaken, or prevent the effect.
- Evidence supporting each important link.
- Outcomes already observed, if any.
- Outcomes that remain hypothetical.

The report must distinguish the fact that an event occurred from the separate question of what consequences it has produced.

### Section 5. Evidence Register or Evidence Summary

**Purpose:** Make the evidential basis of the report inspectable.

The report should identify the key evidence behind its most important claims.

A detailed evidence register may be maintained separately when there is substantial material. In a short report, a concise evidence summary may be sufficient.

Relevant fields include:

- Finding or evidence identifier.
- Claim being assessed.
- Source and location of the supporting material.
- Relevant dates.
- Source status and limitations.
- Evidence status.
- Confidence and rationale.
- Outstanding verification requirements.

Confidence should reflect the quality, directness, consistency, and completeness of the evidence, not merely the number of sources mentioning a claim.

### Section 6. Entity and Relationship Register

**Purpose:** Maintain a structured understanding of the relevant participants and their connections.

This section or its underlying records should identify important entities, their roles, relevant relationships, and the evidence supporting those relationships.

The recurring report need not reproduce the entire register every week. It should highlight newly identified entities, material relationship changes, and connections needed to understand important developments.

Existing records should be updated when the evidence changes rather than duplicated unnecessarily.

### Section 7. Ranked Unresolved Questions

**Purpose:** Make uncertainty explicit and direct further research.

Each question should explain:

- What remains unknown.
- Why the answer matters.
- What evidence is missing.
- Which conclusion or interpretation depends on the answer.
- What research could resolve the uncertainty.
- Its relative priority.

Questions should be ranked according to their importance to the investigation, their effect on the current assessment, and the feasibility of obtaining useful evidence.

This section should distinguish an important unanswered question from a minor detail that does not materially affect the conclusions.

### Section 8. Intelligence-Value Assessment

**Purpose:** Evaluate what the current review contributes to the overall understanding.

This assessment may consider:

- What is newly established.
- Which previous beliefs have been strengthened, weakened, or corrected.
- Which developments materially change the current assessment.
- Which findings are informative but do not change the assessment.
- Where the evidence remains too weak to support a conclusion.
- Whether the research produced a meaningful improvement in understanding.

A report should not imply that substantial new intelligence exists merely because a large amount of information was collected.

A review that establishes no material change can still be useful if its scope and limitations are clear.

### Section 9. Single Best Next Research Step

**Purpose:** Identify the next investigation action most likely to improve the quality or usefulness of the analysis.

The proposed action should:

- Address an important uncertainty or test a consequential relationship.
- Identify the evidence or source required.
- Explain why the step has priority over alternatives.
- State what different possible results would mean for the current assessment.

The aim is to give the next research session a clear starting point without assuming that the proposed step must remain the best option if new evidence emerges.

## 6. How the Reporting Sections Fit Together

The nine sections serve different purposes and should not be treated as nine independent lists of facts.

The executive brief communicates the principal conclusions. The change log records what is different. Priority-event investigations establish what happened and what remains uncertain. Relationship analysis examines possible consequences. The evidence and relationship registers preserve traceability and structure. Unresolved questions expose gaps, the intelligence-value assessment evaluates the contribution of the review, and the next research step directs further investigation.

The sections should refer to one another where useful. For example, a change in the change log may lead to a priority-event investigation, whose findings are recorded in the evidence register and whose possible consequences are examined in the relationship analysis.

This structure is intended to minimise duplication while allowing a reader to move from the overall conclusion to the evidence and reasoning behind it.

## 7. The Relationship Between Weekly Reviews and Deep Dives

A weekly review and a deep dive serve different purposes.

### Weekly review

A weekly review examines the relevant information available during a defined period, compares it with the previous baseline, identifies material changes, updates the assessment, and prioritises unresolved questions.

Its main purpose is to maintain an up-to-date, evidence-backed understanding.

### Deep dive

A deep dive investigates a particular question, event, source, regulation, dataset, mechanism, or uncertainty in greater depth.

It may require clause-by-clause document analysis, detailed calculations, multiple-source verification, alternative explanations, or a longer account of the evidence.

Its purpose is to resolve a question or develop an understanding that the recurring review cannot establish adequately on its own.

### How they connect

A weekly review may identify an event that requires a deep dive. The deep dive then produces findings that can be incorporated into the underlying evidence records and reflected in a later weekly review.

A deep dive should not be treated as a separate, disconnected body of research. Its findings should update the broader investigation where relevant, while preserving the scope and limitations of the deeper analysis.

Likewise, a weekly report should not be overloaded with all the details from every deep dive. It should summarise the important conclusions and direct the reader to the detailed analysis when necessary.

## 8. How the Reporting Approach Should Evolve

The reporting structure should be improved based on actual problems encountered during research.

Potential reasons to revise it include:

- Important claims are difficult to trace to their evidence.
- The same information is repeated in several sections.
- Changes are confused with newly discovered information.
- Possible consequences are presented as established outcomes.
- Uncertainty is hidden or inconsistently described.
- The report contains too much background and too little analysis.
- Important relationships or dependencies are overlooked.
- The next research step does not follow from the unresolved questions.
- The structure is too burdensome for routine use.
- A reader cannot understand the report without an explanation from its author.

For each proposed change, document the problem, the proposed improvement, and the expected benefit.

Do not add a new section merely because it sounds useful. Add it when it serves a distinct purpose that existing sections cannot adequately fulfil.

## 9. Proposed Improvements to Test

The following are recommendations for evaluation, not claims that the existing approach is defective.

### 9.1 Make the scope of each review explicit

Record the period and subject matter examined, the main sources or source categories checked, and important limitations.

This helps readers interpret both positive findings and statements that no material change was established.

### 9.2 Distinguish event status from consequence status

For every important development, distinguish whether the event itself is verified, whether the relevant rule or decision has taken effect, and whether its downstream effects have been observed.

This reduces the risk of treating an announcement, proposal, or plausible mechanism as proof of an implemented change or realised outcome.

### 9.3 Avoid unnecessary duplication

Maintain detailed evidence and relationship records as the underlying reference. Include the relevant findings in the weekly report without copying entire records into multiple sections.

### 9.4 Make confidence explainable

Where confidence assessments are used, explain the basis for them. A confidence label should not substitute for identifying the evidence, limitations, and contradictions.

### 9.5 Link unresolved questions to research actions

Each high-priority unresolved question should have a clear next step or an explanation of why it cannot currently be resolved.

### 9.6 Keep routine reporting proportionate

A review with few material changes should be shorter than a review involving several major developments. The structure should preserve the necessary checks without forcing every section to contain artificial content.

## 10. Quality-Control Checklist for Reports

Before finalising a report, check the following.

### Accuracy and evidence

- Are the main claims supported by identifiable evidence?
- Are source limitations and contradictions visible?
- Are event status, implementation status, and observed outcomes distinguished?

### Change analysis

- Is the comparison baseline clear?
- Are event dates, publication dates, effective dates, and discovery dates distinguished where relevant?
- Are repeated reports about the same event handled consistently?
- Is the significance of each material change explained?

### Interpretation

- Are direct evidence, inference, and hypotheses distinguished?
- Are important relationships and mechanisms examined?
- Are assumptions and missing links stated?
- Are possible consequences separated from observed effects?

### Usefulness

- Does the executive brief communicate the principal conclusions?
- Are priority developments justified?
- Are the most important unresolved questions ranked?
- Does the proposed next step follow from the analysis?

### Proportionality

- Is the report focused on the investigation question?
- Is unnecessary repetition avoided?
- Is the level of detail appropriate to the significance and uncertainty of the findings?
- Can a new reader follow the reasoning without undocumented background knowledge?

Until that comparison is completed, the nine-section version should be treated as a documented working structure, not necessarily the exact original template.

## 12. Intended Outcome

The reporting method should make the investigation easier to understand, evaluate, and continue.

A good report presents the most important conclusions, explains what changed, identifies the evidence, examines why the changes may matter, exposes uncertainty, and directs the next research effort.

The reporting structure should remain adaptable. The underlying investigation principles should stay consistent even when the format, length, subject, or audience changes.
