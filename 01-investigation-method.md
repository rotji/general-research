# Practical Guide to Investigating Information Sources
## A reusable method for finding, examining, verifying and connecting evidence across different fields

**Purpose:** To help researchers, analysts, students, business owners and independent investigators systematically examine information sources and turn scattered information into reliable, useful knowledge.

**Intended use:** This guide can be applied to regulatory research, business analysis, market research, public policy, healthcare, agriculture, finance, education, technology and other fields.

**Place in the framework:** Use the question-and-analysis guide in `02-investigation-question-analysis-structure.md` to determine what needs to be established and which methods are appropriate. This file focuses on how to examine sources and establish evidence reliably. The checklist below remains a practical end-to-end aid, so its steps should be applied in proportion to the question rather than treated as compulsory work for every task. Use `03-reporting-structure-evolution.md` to choose an appropriate output.

# Part 1. The basic principle of source investigation

Research does not end when we find a document.

Finding a document establishes that we have located a potentially useful source. It does not establish that we understand the document, that its claims are correct, that its contents are current, or that its findings apply to our question.

A disciplined investigation has several distinct stages:

1. Find information sources relevant to a clearly defined question.
2. Identify what each source contains and what it can establish.
3. Examine the relevant parts of each source in sufficient detail.
4. Extract specific claims, rules, measurements, relationships or observations.
5. Verify important information against appropriate evidence.
6. Compare sources and identify agreements, contradictions and gaps.
7. Investigate how the findings connect to other people, organisations, processes or events.
8. Determine what the evidence supports, what remains uncertain and what should happen next.

The purpose is not to collect the largest possible number of files. It is to establish what can reliably be learned from the available evidence.

## 1.1 Four things that must remain separate

Throughout an investigation, distinguish these four concepts.

**Source:** Where information was obtained. Examples include a regulator's website, a spreadsheet, a research paper or an interview.

**Claim:** What the source says. For example, a regulator inspected 200 premises.

**Evidence:** The material supporting the claim. This could be an official inspection report, a published table or a documented observation.

**Conclusion:** What the investigator determines after examining the evidence. For example, inspections increased compared with the previous reporting period, provided comparable earlier figures support that conclusion.

A source can contain a claim without providing enough evidence to establish it. A conclusion may be reasonable without being certain. These distinctions should be preserved in research notes and final reports.

# Part 2. Information source categories and how to investigate them

A researcher should recognise different information formats because each format stores information differently and requires different examination techniques.

The following seven categories cover PDF documents, spreadsheets and five additional categories: word-processing documents, presentations, databases and structured data, web pages, and images or scans.

These categories describe formats, not necessarily the authority of a source. A government spreadsheet can be authoritative for one purpose, while an unofficial PDF may contain unverified claims.

## 2.1 PDF documents

### What they are

PDF files are commonly used for regulations, laws, official circulars, technical standards, research papers, annual reports, policy documents, consultation papers, manuals and formal notices.

PDF is a file format, not a document type. Two PDFs may serve entirely different purposes: one might establish a legal obligation, while another merely reports someone's opinion.

### What to investigate

When examining a PDF, establish:

- The title and purpose of the document.
- The issuing organisation or author.
- The publication date, effective date and version, where relevant.
- Whether it is a draft, proposal, final document or historical record.
- Its structure, including sections, headings, definitions, tables, footnotes, annexes and schedules.
- The specific statements or provisions relevant to the research question.
- Any qualifications, exceptions, limitations or conditions.
- Whether a newer version or related document changes the interpretation.

### How to examine it

First, inspect the cover, title, issuing body, date and table of contents. Next, identify the relevant sections rather than reading randomly.

Read the surrounding text before extracting a statement. A sentence may depend on a definition or condition elsewhere in the document. Check footnotes, tables and appendices when they affect the meaning.

If the PDF is a scanned image rather than searchable text, optical character recognition (OCR) may be required. OCR output should be checked against the visible page because recognition errors can alter numbers, names and legal wording.

### Common mistakes

- Assuming every official-looking PDF is final or legally binding.
- Reading only the executive summary.
- Quoting a provision without checking its definitions or exceptions.
- Confusing the publication date with the date a rule takes effect.
- Assuming that publication proves implementation or compliance.

### Example

In a pharmacy investigation, an electronic-pharmacy regulation may establish certain obligations. Separate implementation guidance or enforcement records may be needed to determine how those obligations are being applied in practice.

## 2.2 Spreadsheet workbooks

### What they are

Spreadsheet files include XLSX, XLS, ODS and CSV files. They may contain financial records, prices, sales, inventory, inspections, registrations, survey results, budgets, forecasts or performance indicators.

A workbook may contain multiple sheets, formulas, hidden rows or columns, filters, pivot tables, charts and notes.

A CSV usually contains a single flat table rather than a multi-sheet workbook.

### What to investigate

Examine:

- The meaning of each sheet and column.
- The unit of observation: what does one row represent?
- The time period covered.
- The units used, such as naira, percentages, kilograms or individual transactions.
- The difference between raw data and calculated results.
- Formulas and the cells or ranges they reference.
- Missing values, duplicates, inconsistent labels and unusual values.
- Whether the records represent a complete population or only a sample.
- Whether definitions and measurement methods remained consistent over time.

### How to examine it

Begin by understanding the workbook's structure. Read sheet names, column headings, notes and definitions before calculating anything.

Identify the grain of the data. For example, a row might represent one pharmacy, one product, one monthly transaction or one inspection. Confusing these levels can produce incorrect totals.

Check formulas against sample records and independently recompute important totals when possible. Investigate missing data, duplicate records and changes in measurement before interpreting trends.

### Common mistakes

- Assuming a displayed total is correct because it is in a spreadsheet.
- Adding percentages that have different denominators.
- Comparing figures from different periods without checking definitions.
- Treating an empty cell as zero without evidence.
- Treating a forecast as an actual result.
- Confusing correlation with causation.

### Example

Suppose a workbook shows that medicine prices increased. Before concluding that pharmacies face higher procurement costs, establish whether the data represents wholesale prices, retail prices, advertised online prices or actual completed transactions.

## 2.3 Word-processing documents

### Common formats

DOCX, DOC and ODT.

### What they are

Word-processing files are used for reports, draft policies, contracts, meeting minutes, internal procedures, proposals, formal correspondence and consultation submissions.

They may contain information that is not obvious from the visible paragraphs, such as tracked changes, comments, footnotes and document metadata.

### What to investigate

Look for:

- The document's author, recipient and purpose.
- Whether the document is a draft, submitted proposal, approved version or signed agreement.
- Commitments, responsibilities and proposed actions.
- Conditions, exceptions, deadlines and approval requirements.
- Tracked changes and comments that identify unresolved issues.
- References to supporting evidence or other documents.
- Differences between successive versions.

### How to examine it

Read the title, date, authorship and status first. Then examine the relevant sections and any appendices.

If revision history is important, inspect tracked changes and comments rather than assuming the visible text represents the complete decision process. Treat metadata as supporting information, not conclusive proof of authorship or authenticity.

### Common mistakes

- Treating a draft proposal as an approved policy.
- Treating a meeting note as proof that a proposed action was completed.
- Ignoring changes between versions.
- Assuming a person's signature alone establishes the validity of the entire document.

### Example

An industry association's submission may identify concerns about a proposed law. It establishes the association's stated position, but does not prove that the legislature accepted the recommendations.

## 2.4 Presentation files

### Common formats

PPTX, PPT and ODP.

### What they are

Presentations are often used for executive briefings, policy announcements, investment proposals, conference talks, business reviews and project updates.

They tend to summarise information rather than document every supporting detail.

### What to investigate

Look for:

- Stated objectives and strategic priorities.
- Targets, milestones and timelines.
- Charts, figures and comparisons.
- Definitions, units, dates and footnotes.
- The distinction between past achievements and future ambitions.
- Assumptions underlying forecasts.
- Claims that require verification from a more detailed source.
- Information that is omitted from the presentation but necessary to evaluate its claims.

### How to examine it

Inspect the title and date, then review each relevant slide alongside its charts, notes and footnotes.

For important numerical claims, seek the underlying dataset or report. For targets, look for actual results. For forecasts, identify the assumptions and conditions on which they depend.

### Common mistakes

- Treating a target as an achieved outcome.
- Accepting a chart without checking its axis, units or baseline.
- Comparing percentages calculated from different populations.
- Treating a persuasive presentation as a complete evidence record.

### Example

A presentation stating that domestic manufacturing should supply 70% of national medicine demand expresses a target or ambition unless it also provides evidence that this level has been achieved.

## 2.5 Databases and structured data

### Common formats

CSV, JSON, XML, SQL exports and records retrieved from databases or application programming interfaces (APIs).

### What they are

Structured data stores information in fields and records. It may describe registered businesses, licensed professionals, medicine products, transactions, inspections, prices, shipments or safety alerts.

Structured records are particularly useful for investigating patterns across many observations.

### What to investigate

Establish:

- What each record represents.
- What each field means and which units it uses.
- How records are identified and linked.
- Whether records cover the entire population or only part of it.
- Whether the data is current.
- How missing values and duplicates are represented.
- Whether the dataset contains historical records or only the latest state.
- Whether fields or definitions changed over time.
- What access, collection or sampling limitations apply.

### How to examine it

Read the schema, data dictionary or field descriptions if available. Inspect sample records before analysing the full dataset.

Determine the unit of analysis, check record identifiers and assess missingness and duplication. If joining multiple datasets, verify that the keys actually identify the same entities and that the join does not multiply or lose records.

### Common mistakes

- Assuming every database record is accurate or current.
- Joining datasets on similar-looking but incompatible identifiers.
- Counting records as if they necessarily represent unique people, products or businesses.
- Ignoring selection bias and incomplete coverage.
- Interpreting a database's absence of a record as proof that something does not exist.

### Example

A public register might identify licensed premises. It does not necessarily establish whether each premise is currently operating, fully compliant or adequately stocked unless the dataset records those conditions.

## 2.6 Web pages and archived web pages

### What they are

Web pages include official notices, regulatory portals, announcements, FAQs, public registers, company disclosures, news reports, guidance pages and links to downloadable documents.

Unlike a fixed document, a web page may change without retaining its earlier text.

### What to investigate

Record:

- The page title and URL.
- The organisation responsible for publishing it.
- The publication date, if available.
- The date the investigator accessed it.
- Any stated update or revision date.
- Links to underlying documents.
- Whether the page describes a proposal, a decision or an implemented action.
- Whether an earlier version differs from the current one.

### How to examine it

Read the full relevant page rather than relying only on a search-engine snippet. Follow links to primary documents where possible.

For time-sensitive research, record when the page was accessed. If a change matters, compare earlier and current versions when reliable archives or dated copies exist.

### Common mistakes

- Confusing the date an article was published with the date an event occurred.
- Treating an updated page as proof that a policy itself changed.
- Relying on search snippets without checking the underlying page.
- Assuming that a page with no recent entries proves no recent events occurred.

### Example

A regulator's publications page may newly list a regulation that was launched months earlier. The listing is newly observed evidence; the regulation's original launch is not a new event.

---

## 2.7 Images, scans and photographs

### What they are

Images may include photographs of official notices, scanned letters, screenshots, charts, product labels, receipts, certificates and physical records.

Some image files contain text that cannot be searched directly.

### What to investigate

Look for:

- Visible text, dates, names and reference numbers.
- Product names, batch numbers and expiry dates, where relevant.
- Signs of missing or cropped context.
- Whether the image is a complete page or an excerpt.
- Whether OCR accurately captured the text.
- Whether the original document or issuing organisation can be located.
- Whether the image's context and provenance can be established.

### How to examine it

Preserve the original image. Transcribe relevant text carefully and check OCR against the visible content.

For important claims, seek the complete original document or an authoritative copy. If the image is cropped, record that limitation rather than reconstructing missing text from guesswork.

### Common mistakes

- Assuming a screenshot is complete.
- Treating an image of a signature or stamp as conclusive proof of authenticity.
- Ignoring possible cropping or editing.
- Misreading numbers or names in low-quality scans.

### Example

A photograph of a medicine recall notice may identify a product and batch. Before advising a retailer to withdraw stock, verify the notice against the regulator's official alert and check that the batch details match.

# Part 3. Understand what kind of source you have found

File format and source type are different.

A regulation can be a PDF. An annual report can be a PDF or a web page. A database can be published as a spreadsheet. A proposal can be circulated as a Word document or a presentation.

Before analysing a source, classify its purpose and status.

Common source purposes include:

- **Law or regulation:** establishes legal rules, subject to the relevant legal framework.
- **Guideline or standard:** explains recommended or required practices, depending on its authority and wording.
- **Policy or strategy:** states objectives, priorities or planned interventions.
- **Proposal or draft:** presents changes that may not have been approved.
- **Operational record:** records activities, inspections, transactions or decisions.
- **Statistical dataset:** provides observations or measurements.
- **Research study:** reports a question, method, findings and limitations.
- **Public announcement:** communicates a position, decision or intended action.
- **Advocacy submission:** presents an organisation's position or recommendations.
- **News report:** reports events and claims, sometimes based on interviews or other documents.
- **Form or checklist:** specifies information or steps required for a process.

These categories can overlap. A source may serve several purposes, and its authority must be assessed in context.

## 3.1 Establish source status

For each important source, ask:

1. Who issued it?
2. What authority or expertise does the issuer have for this subject?
3. Is it final, draft, proposed, historical or superseded?
4. What date does it relate to?
5. What exactly does it establish?
6. What does it not establish?
7. Is there a more authoritative or complete source?

Do not automatically rank all government sources above all other sources. A government page may establish an official publication date, while a company filing may be the best evidence of that company's reported results. Authority depends on the claim being investigated.

# Part 4. Reusable investigation checklist

This checklist is intended for use by a researcher who has not read this guide before. Each step explains what to do, why it matters, how to do it and what a satisfactory result looks like.

## Step 1. Define the research question

**Meaning:** State the specific uncertainty the investigation is intended to resolve.

**Why it matters:** Without a defined question, researchers can accumulate large amounts of interesting but irrelevant material.

**How to do it:**

- Identify the subject, location and period.
- State what you want to establish.
- Specify the decision or conclusion the research may support.
- Identify what would count as a useful answer.

**Example:** Instead of asking, “What is happening in Nigerian pharmacy?”, ask, “Have any new national requirements affecting independent retail pharmacies been published or brought into force between 18 and 25 October 2026?”

**Completion test:** Another person can read the question and understand what information is relevant and what falls outside the scope.

## Step 2. Identify and record the source

**Meaning:** Create a traceable record of the material being examined.

**Why it matters:** A finding is difficult to verify if nobody can locate the source again.

**How to do it:** Record the title, issuer or author, source type, URL or file location, publication date, access date and version where available.

**Example:** “Electronic Pharmacy Regulations 2026; Pharmacy Council of Nigeria; official regulation; PCN publications page; accessed 25 October 2026; PDF version to be checked.”

**Completion test:** Another researcher can locate the same source or understand precisely which version was used.

## Step 3. Establish the document's status and context

**Meaning:** Determine what kind of source it is and whether it represents a draft, proposal, final rule, historical report or current dataset.

**Why it matters:** The same words have different implications depending on whether they describe a proposal or an action that has already taken effect.

**How to do it:** Examine the cover page, issuing authority, date, version information, approval status and relevant notices. Compare with a newer or older version if the status is unclear.

**Completion test:** You can state what the source is, when it applies, and what remains uncertain about its status.

## Step 4. Identify the source's internal structure

**Meaning:** Locate the parts that organise and explain the information.

**Why it matters:** Important details may be found in definitions, annexes, footnotes, data dictionaries or appendices rather than the summary.

**How to do it:**

- In PDFs, inspect headings, sections, definitions, tables and schedules.
- In spreadsheets, inspect sheets, column names, formulas and notes.
- In presentations, inspect slides, charts and footnotes.
- In databases, inspect schemas, identifiers and field descriptions.
- In web pages, inspect linked documents, notices and update dates.

**Completion test:** You can explain where the relevant evidence is located and why those parts matter.

## Step 5. Extract the exact evidence

**Meaning:** Record the relevant statement, provision, measurement or observation without changing its meaning.

**Why it matters:** Summaries can unintentionally strengthen, weaken or distort the original claim.

**How to do it:** Preserve important wording, numbers, dates, units and references. Record the page, section, clause, table, sheet, row or field when possible.

Separate direct quotations from paraphrases and your own interpretations.

**Completion test:** A reviewer can return to the cited location and confirm what the source actually says.

## Step 6. Check definitions, scope and exceptions

**Meaning:** Determine precisely what the claim or requirement applies to and under what conditions.

**Why it matters:** A rule for one category of organisation may not apply to another. A statistic may cover a sample rather than an entire population.

**How to do it:** Search for definitions, qualifying language, exclusions, exemptions, thresholds, footnotes and cross-references. Record who or what is included and excluded.

**Completion test:** You can state the scope of the finding without implying that it applies more broadly than the evidence allows.

## Step 7. Check numbers, methods and calculations

**Meaning:** Establish how quantitative findings were produced and whether they can be reproduced.

**Why it matters:** Incorrect denominators, inconsistent units, missing data or spreadsheet errors can invalidate a conclusion.

**How to do it:** Identify the measurement, unit, period, population and calculation method. Recompute key figures where possible and check for duplicates, missingness and inconsistent definitions.

**Completion test:** The figure has a clear meaning and calculation, or its limitations are explicitly recorded.

## Step 8. Verify the claim against appropriate evidence

**Meaning:** Determine whether the source's important claims are sufficiently supported.

**Why it matters:** A report can repeat an inaccurate claim, and several articles can all rely on the same original report.

**How to do it:** Look for the underlying primary document, independent corroboration or a reliable alternative source. Compare dates, numbers and descriptions. Investigate disagreements instead of silently choosing one version.

**Completion test:** Each important claim has an identifiable evidence basis and a stated level of confidence.

## Step 9. Compare versions and time periods

**Meaning:** Establish what changed and when it changed.

**Why it matters:** A newly discovered document may describe an old event, and a changed web page may not reflect a changed policy.

**How to do it:** Record the event date, publication date, discovery date and access date where applicable. Compare previous and current documents, datasets or records. Identify additions, deletions, revisions and unchanged items.

**Completion test:** You can distinguish a genuinely new event from newly discovered information, a changed source and a changed interpretation.

## Step 10. Trace dependencies and relationships

**Meaning:** Identify the people, organisations, resources, rules and processes connected to the finding.

**Why it matters:** A source may describe only one part of a system. Understanding dependencies can explain why an event matters beyond its immediate subject.

**How to do it:** Ask who is directly affected, who controls the relevant process, what must happen first, what resources are required and which other parties may be affected.

**Example:** A change in medicine import policy could affect importers, but its impact on pharmacies may depend on alternative supply, distributor inventory, product availability and wholesale pricing.

**Completion test:** The relationship is either supported by evidence or explicitly marked as a hypothesis requiring verification.

## Step 11. Test consequences rather than assuming them

**Meaning:** Determine which consequences are observed and which are merely plausible.

**Why it matters:** A policy change does not automatically produce the intended outcome, and an upstream cost change does not prove a downstream price increase.

**How to do it:** Build a causal explanation, identify the intermediate steps, and look for evidence at each important step. Record alternative explanations and missing evidence.

**Completion test:** The conclusion separates confirmed effects from likely mechanisms and untested possibilities.

## Step 12. Record gaps and the next research question

**Meaning:** Document what the investigation could not establish and what evidence would resolve the uncertainty.

**Why it matters:** Explicit gaps prevent uncertain assumptions from becoming accepted facts in later research.

**How to do it:** For each unresolved question, record why it matters, what is currently known, what is missing, where the missing evidence may exist and how feasible it is to obtain.

**Completion test:** The next researcher can continue from the existing work without repeating the entire investigation or mistaking uncertainty for absence.

# Part 5. Deeper elements to investigate across different fields

The checklist describes the investigation process. The following concepts describe the kinds of information that may be hidden within a source or its surrounding system.

Not every concept applies to every source. Use the ones relevant to the research question.

## 5.1 Definitions and scope

Find how key terms are defined and determine which entities, products, locations, periods or activities are included.

**Question:** Does the source mean what an ordinary reader might assume it means?

## 5.2 Dependencies and prerequisites

Find what must be available, approved or completed before another action can occur.

**Question:** What must happen first, and who or what does the outcome depend on?

## 5.3 Dates, deadlines and transition periods

Separate the date an event occurred, the date it was reported, the date a document was issued and the date a requirement becomes applicable.

**Question:** When did the change actually occur, and when does it affect the relevant party?

## 5.4 Exceptions and edge cases

Find situations in which a general rule does not apply or applies differently.

**Question:** Who or what is treated differently, and under what conditions?

## 5.5 Numbers, thresholds and formulas

Find the underlying measurement, denominator, calculation and trigger for a decision.

**Question:** What exactly is being measured, and can the result be independently reproduced?

## 5.6 Incentives, costs and allocation of risk

Investigate who pays, who benefits, who bears the risk and what behaviour the arrangement encourages.

**Question:** How might the arrangement change the decisions of the people or organisations involved?

## 5.7 Implementation and enforcement

Distinguish announcement, proposal, approval, publication, commencement, implementation, enforcement and demonstrated effectiveness.

**Question:** What evidence shows that the intended change has actually reached the real world?

## 5.8 Version history and change over time

Compare earlier and later versions to identify what changed, when and with what stated reason.

**Question:** Is this a new development, an old event reported again or a change in how the information is presented?

## 5.9 Evidence quality and missing information

Investigate authorship, source authority, methods, corroboration, selection bias and limitations.

**Question:** What does the available evidence establish, and what cannot yet be concluded?

# Part 6. A standard method for recording findings

The investigation should produce records that other people can review, challenge and update.

For every significant finding, record:

| Field | What to enter |
|---|---|
| Finding ID | A unique identifier that remains stable when the record is updated |
| Research question | The question this finding helps answer |
| Claim | The precise statement being evaluated |
| Source | Issuer, title and URL or file reference |
| Source location | Page, section, clause, table, sheet, row or field |
| Relevant dates | Event date, publication date, discovery date and access date as applicable |
| Source status | Draft, proposal, final, historical, current or uncertain |
| Evidence status | Confirmed, supported, disputed, inferred or unverified |
| Confidence | How strongly the evidence supports the claim and why |
| Scope and limitations | What is included, excluded or not established |
| Relationships | Relevant people, organisations, processes or dependencies |
| Follow-up | The unresolved question or evidence needed next |

Do not overwrite an earlier conclusion without preserving what changed. If a new source changes the interpretation, record the new evidence and explain why the conclusion changed.

For recurring research, useful change classifications include:

- **NEW_WORLD_EVENT:** A new real-world development occurred.
- **NEWLY_DISCOVERED:** Information about an earlier development was found during the current investigation.
- **SOURCE_CHANGED:** A source, page or document changed.
- **INTERPRETATION_CHANGED:** New evidence changed the meaning or significance assigned to existing information.
- **NO_MATERIAL_CHANGE:** The checked evidence did not establish a material development.
- **UNVERIFIED:** The claim could not be adequately confirmed or rejected.

These classifications describe different situations. For example, an old regulation newly discovered in an archive is not a new real-world event.

# Part 7. A worked example

The following example is illustrative and does not assert any new fact about a particular Nigerian pharmacy.

**Research question:** Has a new regulatory requirement affected a particular category of retail pharmacy?

**Step 1 — Locate sources.** Find the official regulation, relevant guidance and any implementation notice.

**Step 2 — Establish status.** Determine whether the document is a draft, final rule or historical version.

**Step 3 — Examine the relevant provisions.** Locate definitions, obligations, exceptions, effective dates and penalties.

**Step 4 — Extract evidence.** Record the exact provisions and their locations.

**Step 5 — Verify interpretation.** Check cross-references and later official guidance.

**Step 6 — Trace implementation.** Look for evidence that the requirement has taken effect and applies to the pharmacy category in question.

**Step 7 — Investigate consequences.** Determine what actions are required and whether there is evidence of actual operational or financial effects.

**Step 8 — Record uncertainty.** If the rule's application to a particular business model remains unclear, preserve the question instead of guessing.

The result is more useful than a summary saying that a new regulation exists. It explains what the source establishes, who may be affected, how to verify the effect and what remains unknown.

# Part 8. Final quality-control questions

Before sharing an investigation with another person, check the following:

1. Can the reader understand the research question without additional explanation?
2. Can every important factual claim be traced to its source?
3. Are quotations, numbers, dates and units accurate?
4. Have drafts, proposals and final decisions been distinguished?
5. Have relevant definitions, exceptions, footnotes and supporting tables been checked?
6. Are the evidence and interpretation clearly separated?
7. Are causal claims supported by evidence for the important intermediate steps?
8. Have contradictions and missing information been recorded?
9. Can another researcher reproduce the important calculations or verify the relevant source locations?
10. Does the report explain what remains unknown and identify the next useful research step?

If these questions can be answered satisfactorily, the work is ready for review or handover.

# Conclusion

The central skill is not simply locating information. It is learning how to examine a source at the right level of detail, establish what its contents mean, verify important claims, connect the evidence to the wider system and record uncertainty honestly.

PDFs, spreadsheets, word-processing files, presentations, structured data, web pages and images are different containers for information. Their formats influence how they should be examined, but the underlying discipline remains the same.

A good investigation leaves behind more than a collection of links. It produces a traceable body of evidence that another person can understand, verify, challenge, update and use to make a better decision.
